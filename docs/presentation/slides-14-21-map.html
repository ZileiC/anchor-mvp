14 17:00–18:00 45 s · ~100 words

Windows host and Android client

CoreOne laptop is the whole product; the phone is a client that reads, never listens.

What is on the slide

  • hostFlutter senior interface plus FastAPI and SQLite on one Windows laptop
  • linkTrusted local network, HTTP on TCP 8077; discovery on UDP 8078
  • clientAndroid caregiver app: family setup, approved report
  • costThe host must stay on; manual approval needs no worker

Why it follows slide 13

The prototype conversation is now a finished picture. This slide says where that picture physically lives, because architecture is what makes the rest of the block checkable — the data stays in the home, so the report has to be pulled, not pushed.

Bridge to 15“That is the shape. Now inside that host — how a sentence gets in and a voice gets out.”

Say this45 s

Everything sits on one machine. The Windows laptop runs two things: the Flutter interface the older adult touches, and the backend beside it — FastAPI with a SQLite database. The Android phone is a client. It asks the backend for the approved report and shows it; it never opens a microphone.

They talk over one trusted local network on TCP 8077, with UDP 8078 for automatic discovery — and if discovery fails, the family can type the address by hand.

Two honest costs of keeping the data in the home: the host has to stay on and reachable, and a scheduled weekly report needs a separate worker. Manual approval needs none.

15 18:00–19:00 60 s · ~110 words

Speech, captions and fallback

CoreEvery stage has a visible fallback, so a failure costs a step, not the conversation.

Pipeline, in order

  • inputRecognition — local or configured cloud recogniser turns audio into text
  • turnConversation — the backend writes a reply; typing bypasses capture
  • outputVoice — DeepSeek, Edge, or the device voice, whichever is configured
  • recoveryCaptions stay; Stop always works; retry once reachable

Where the logic goes

Slide 14 gave a box diagram. This one opens the box and shows the only thing that matters for an older adult: what happens when it breaks. The four rows are a sequence, but the fourth row is the argument — it is the claim that failures were designed for rather than discovered.

Bridge to 16“We can describe that pipeline. The next two slides are about whether it actually holds.”

Say this60 s

Four stages. Speech recognition turns audio into text, locally or through a configured cloud recogniser. Conversation takes that text — or typed text, which skips capture entirely — and the backend writes a reply. Voice output speaks it: the configured path can use DeepSeek, Microsoft Edge, or the device voice. And recovery: the caption stays on screen while audio is being prepared, Stop works at any point, and a retry is one tap once the service is back.

That is the design claim: an older adult should never be left looking at a dead screen. The screen keeps telling them what is happening.

16 19:00–20:00 60 s · ~110 words

What the backend smoke checked

CoreWe wrote a repeatable check, and it tests the four things most likely to break quietly.

Eight groups, four headlines

  • rolesHealth endpoint answered; a caregiver could not change consent (403)
  • turnSynthetic voice turn returned a reply; Stop worked after revocation
  • reportDraft 403 → approved readable → revoked highlights filtered
  • failureSimulated outage 503, then a typed retry succeeded

Relation between the four

Not four features — four failure modes: who is allowed to change consent, whether Stop still works after revocation, whether an unapproved report leaks, and whether a dead provider ends the conversation. Each one has an observable status code on the slide.

Bridge to 17“Same script, two machines.”

Say this60 s

It is eight groups of checks against a real local socket and a temporary database, with synthetic records and test providers.

Startup and roles — the health endpoint answered, and a caregiver could not change the older adult's consent settings. Conversation and Stop — a synthetic voice turn returned a reply, and Stop still worked after consent was revoked. Report access — a caregiver asking for the draft got a 403; after the older adult approved it, the report was readable, and revoked highlights disappeared. Provider failure — a simulated outage returned a 503, and a typed retry succeeded.

The saved Windows evidence is on the slide.

Bridge to 18“Which brings us to what a small pilot would actually need.”

Say this45 s

The same script passed all eight groups on macOS and on Windows. The full automated suites — 442 backend tests, 319 Flutter tests, run on macOS — cover the rest of the behaviour.

What this does not prove: a real microphone, a real speaker, or a real phone on a real network. Those are device questions, and they are the next two slides.

18 20:45–21:30 40 s · ~85 words

What a classroom pilot needs

CoreFour requirements, all of them people or procedures — none of them new code.

The four

  • hostOne reachable Windows laptop; network rehearsed
  • failureSynthetic speech, typed input, prepared walkthrough
  • reportSomeone seeds last week and checks the first report
  • scheduleManual works; scheduled generation needs an owner

Why this is the turning point

Everything so far was about the system. This slide turns to operating it — and quietly makes the case that feasibility is organisational, not technical. Say “we already own the equipment” out loud; that is the answer to the feasibility question.

Bridge to 19“Cost is the other half of feasibility.”

Say this40 s

A reachable Windows host: service and database on one laptop, with the network rehearsed. A way to handle provider failure: synthetic speech, typed input, a prepared walkthrough if the network drops. A report operator: seed the last completed week and check the first report before anyone watches. And, for anything ongoing, a schedule owner — manual publishing works without a worker; scheduled generation and retention need one.

That is the honest bar for “feasible in a classroom”: we already own the equipment, and we can name the four jobs.

19 21:30–22:05 35 s · ~85 words

Illustrative monthly API costs

CoreAbout one and a half dollars a month for the API — a scenario, not a bill.

Numbers on the slide

  • usage10 cloud turns/day × 30 days
  • per turn1,000 in / 100 out tokens, 50 Chinese characters of speech
  • total≈ US$1.48 — about $0.13 chat, $1.35 speech
  • excludedElectricity, connectivity, storage, support, retries

How to say a number honestly

Give the figure, then the assumptions, then what it excludes — in that order, and never call it a bill. The moment you say “measured”, someone can ask what the invoice said, and there isn’t one. Order of magnitude is the claim; quote is not.

Bridge to 20“Cheap and feasible are not the same as safe to distribute — and that is the last thing I want to be precise about.”

Say this35 s

Under stated assumptions — ten cloud turns a day for thirty days, each turn one thousand input tokens, one hundred output tokens, fifty Chinese characters of speech — the API subtotal is about US$1.48 per household-month. Roughly eight cents of that is chat, one dollar thirty-five is speech.

It excludes electricity, connectivity, storage, support and retries, and nobody has been billed for this. The arithmetic and the dated rates are in the feasibility report; treat the number as an order of magnitude, not a quote.

The slide's own cost line mentions ASR inside a free allowance. The speech-recognition service actually used is a paid one, so keep the sentence above as written — it claims chat and speech synthesis only — or change that line before the presentation.

Why this is the strongest slide

Everything before this is capability. This is the slide that makes the capability believable, because it names what is not solved and points at a link instead of shrugging. Deliver it flatly — no apology, no rush.

Bridge to 21“So the last slide before the demo is the gate we hold ourselves to.”

Say this30 s

Cloud opt-out: the direct speech endpoints need a server-side consent check before they call an external provider — that gap is open. Deployment boundary: this is a classroom build — demo identities, plain LAN HTTP, synthetic records only.

Both open items are pull requests, and neither is hidden.

Five answers, kept short

open the one you are asked
Is this a medical device?

No. It is a companion for conversation and a weekly report. It never diagnoses, scores cognition or gives medical advice — that constraint is written into the prompt the model runs under, not just the interface.

Why not just one app on the older adult's phone?

Because the family phone is where settings live, and one screen with one Talk button is the whole design for the person using it. Splitting the product was simpler than hiding controls from them.

Why a laptop instead of the cloud?

Privacy and cost. The database stays in the home and the API subtotal is about a dollar and a half a month. The price is that the laptop has to be on — we say that out loud rather than hide it.

What happens if the network dies mid-sentence?

The caption stays, Stop keeps working, and the retry is one tap. The typed path never needs the microphone.

Did real older adults use this?

Not yet, and we do not claim it. The participant protocol exists and the observation sheet is empty — a deliberate gap, not an oversight.
